02 · How a good engagement works

Automation is a practice, not a purchase

The automations that last share five traits. Any proposal that skips one of them tends to break quietly a few months in.

  • Objectives

    A stated business outcome

    Faster replies, cleaner handoffs, fewer dropped tasks. If the goal is "use AI", the project has no finish line.

  • Selection

    The right workflow

    High-volume, rule-shaped, repetitive work automates well. Ambiguous judgement calls usually belong with people.

  • Oversight

    Human review where it matters

    Automation handles the volume; a person still approves anything that touches a customer relationship or a commitment.

  • Measurement

    Operational outcomes

    Hours returned, response times, error rates — tracked before and after, so the result is an observation rather than a promise.

  • Reliability

    Maintenance and monitoring

    Tools change and workflows drift. Automations need an owner, alerting, and a scheduled review — or they rot.

03 · Use cases

Four places automation usually pays off first

These are the workflows we see most often. Each one has a concrete shape, and each one is measured before anyone builds anything.

Lead qualification

Every inbound enquiry gets a consistent first response, a short qualifying question set, and a routed, summarised handoff — so sales time goes to conversations, not triage.

Example A form submission is scored against your criteria, answered within a minute, and booked straight into the right calendar.

Customer support triage

Tickets are classified, tagged, and matched to the right article or owner. Repetitive questions get a drafted reply a human approves rather than one typed from scratch.

Example A shipping enquiry is answered with the tracking status pulled from your order system, no agent involved.

Repetitive admin

Invoicing, data entry, file handoffs, spreadsheet copy-paste. Unglamorous work with a clear rule set and a measurable time cost — usually the fastest win.

Example A weekly report is assembled from three tools and emailed every Friday at 16:00, without anyone opening a tab.

Internal reporting

Operational numbers gathered from the tools your team already uses, compiled on a schedule, and delivered where people actually read them.

Example A Monday digest of pipeline changes, overdue tasks, and support load, generated from your existing systems.

04 · Process

From first conversation to a running automation

An example of how an engagement runs. These are the steps of the service this website advertises — not features of the website template itself.

  1. Step 1

    Discover the workflow

    We walk the task with the people who do it, note the rules they apply, and record the volume and time it costs per week.

  2. Step 2

    Design the automation

    You see the proposed flow, the data it touches, and the review points before anything is built. Scope and success measures are agreed here.

  3. Step 3

    Implement and validate

    The automation is built against real-but-safe inputs, tested with the edge cases your work actually produces, and handed over with documentation.

  4. Step 4

    Monitor and improve

    Failures alert a named owner, results are compared against the baseline, and the workflow is revisited as your tools change.

08 · Questions

Frequently asked questions

Straight answers to the questions that come up in a first conversation.

What kinds of tasks are suitable for automation?

Work that is frequent, follows rules you can write down, and uses data your tools already produce. Qualification routing, status follow-ups, report assembly, and data transfer between systems are typical. One-off judgement calls usually are not worth automating.

How should a business estimate potential savings?

Start from measured time: how often the task runs, how long one run takes, and what that hour costs you fully loaded. Multiply through, subtract the recurring cost of running the automation, and treat the result as an estimate — the calculator above follows exactly this method. Real savings show up in the measurement after implementation, not in the estimate before it.

What happens when an automation fails?

Failures are expected, which is why every automation we ship has alerting to a named person and a run history you can inspect. A failed step is paused rather than repeated blindly, so a bad input never produces a flood of wrong actions.

Is human review still necessary?

Usually, for anything a customer or a partner sees, and for anything with money or commitments attached. A good automation does the volume and the drafting; a person approves what matters. Work that is purely internal and reversible can often run unattended.

How long might an implementation take?

It depends on how many systems the workflow touches and how reliable their data is. A single-tool automation can be days; something spanning three systems with messy inputs is more likely weeks. An audit exists precisely to replace a guess with a scoped answer before anyone builds.

Who owns the automation afterwards?

You do. Automations are documented and built in your accounts wherever the tooling allows it, so nothing depends on us staying involved. A retainer is available for monitoring and fixes, but it is optional.
